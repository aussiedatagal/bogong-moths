Year of the Bogong

This spring, swarms of endangered Bogong moths turned up in homes and gardens across south-east Australia.

Each year, nature lovers share their photos on the iNaturalist app. Scientists use them as crowdsourced field work, mostly to map where animals and plants live.

Each dot is a Bogong someone photographed this spring.

Adjusted for where people live, Bogongs stood out most in the Blue Mountains and the Illawarra, with Sydney and the South Coast close behind.

Around Sydney, people have photographed 1,728 Bogongs so far this spring. In previous years, Bogongs made up fewer than 1 in 40 moths photographed there. So why are we seeing so many?

To answer that, let's start with where Bogongs live and how they migrate. They breed over winter on the inland plains. Each spring they fly up to 1,000 km to cool caves in the Snowy Mountains and Victoria's high country, navigating on instinct by the stars and the Earth's magnetic field.

This autumn and winter, rain over the breeding grounds was a third above average. Bogong caterpillars live in the soil there and feed on the plants that come up after rain, so the moths likely had a baby boom of sorts.

So how did they end up in Sydney, when it's not on their flight path? Experts think strong north-westerly winds high up blew many off course toward the coast, and city lights drew them down into our homes.

Still endangered

The drought from 2017 crashed the population. In the mountains, caves that once held hundreds of thousands of moths held only a few, according to a 2021 study, and the IUCN listed the Bogong as endangered that year.

This spring's swarms are a hopeful sign, but nobody has counted how many moths made it.

If Bogongs turn up at your place, Zoos Victoria's Marissa Parrott suggests switching off outdoor lights, keeping cats inside and gently ushering any moths back out. They won't stay long. They have mountains to get to.
